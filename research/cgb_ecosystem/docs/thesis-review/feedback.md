**Extracted reasoning.** A sequential predictor is part of a dynamical system when its decisions change future observations, available actions, or outcomes. Several distinct feedback channels can coexist. They require different experiments and cannot be diagnosed from one static accuracy number. Repeated fitting alone does not imply endogenous feedback. A stable fitted model can remain wrong, and a useful intervention can make its original forecast look wrong.

**Derived decision.** Preserve the current frozen experiment as a constructive baseline. Build a separate, explicitly synthetic experimental layer with independently switchable observation, action, adaptation, and delayed-outcome mechanisms. Use it to test what must be true for the existing one-hour advantage to survive. Prioritize identifying the successful component and mapping its failure boundary before adding a large adaptive controller.

**Uncertainties and contradictions.** The supplied text is sufficient to reconstruct mechanisms and assumptions, but some formulas, figure layouts, and symbols are damaged by extraction. This is full-text reading, not independent theorem verification, visual checking of original PDFs, replication of the papers, or real-market validation. Source-repository mathematical conventions are not automatically requirements for the CAD model. No existing code, report, result, or thesis file was changed.

## What the current study has already chosen well

The current study asks a bounded sufficiency question: in constructed linked rates markets with persistent adjustment, can a specified predictor extract useful continuation? Independent TRAIN/CAL/TEST roles, frozen stress fits, and causal receiver timestamps make that question answerable. They prevent feedback from being accidentally confused with future leakage or retrospective selection. The generator and predictor are separate constructions; that is useful even though their economic hypotheses can still align by design. The model's observable state labels are expressly descriptions, not recovered latent causes. This matches the construct-versus-measurement distinctions in papers 17 and 21.

Probability, path risk, and execution are evaluated separately. Price-touch accounting, next-minute entry, visible unavailable outcomes, and withholding an entire book's aggregate when an exit remains unpriced preserve the meaning of results. Exact weighted sums over a finite scenario bank are an appropriate numerical choice. Simulation randomness is valuable for alternative worlds; resampling an already enumerated finite bank is unnecessary to calculate its exact moments.

The reported one-hour advantage belongs, at the displayed precision, to a mixture with supervised weight 1.000 in all three base seeds (REPORT lines 278, 284, 290). Thus evidence for useful one-hour direction does not establish that every state or local-neighbor construction contributed to that result. The empirical path bank still supplies conditional magnitudes and adverse paths. A fruitful next question is what the path layer adds beyond the endpoint classifier, rather than treating the entire architecture as a single validated object.

The fast-decay stress is especially informative because it keeps TRAIN/CAL and the fit fixed while changing TEST persistence. It exposes sensitivity to an assumed mechanism. It does not uniquely identify unfinished adjustment: altered autocorrelation, feature reliability, and support mismatch can also produce the observed loss. The pressure-removed control is not a strict martingale control because other structured effects remain. Neither point makes simulation useless; both identify better counterfactuals to construct now.

## What should be added conceptually

Separate five quantities in every future adaptive experiment: latent market state, observable messages, forecast, chosen action, and subsequently available label. Add policy state and the pending-outcome queue as separate objects. A forecast made at time t for t+240 cannot update a model or governor at t+60. Receiver availability and outcome maturity are distinct clocks. Report censoring counts and outstanding exposure alongside each diagnostic.

Distinguish at least three targets: forecast quality for a passive observer; forecast quality on the distribution caused by a deployed policy; and the total economic outcome of that policy, including costs, drawdown, inventory, and lost opportunities. The current study primarily estimates the first and a simplified price-taking version of the third. A better score for the second can result from making the world easier to predict rather than discovering a better signal.

Do not equate every market with a high-power platform. Begin the own-impact and visibility sweeps at exactly zero. A one-contract private predictor may reasonably approximate a price taker. A public signal copied by many agents or an aggregated large strategy may not. The strength and channel of influence should be parameters, not philosophical assumptions.

## Document-by-document review

### 13. Cybernetic Governance in a Coliving House

Daniel Kronovet, Seth Frey, and Joseph DeSimone. [Source](coverage.md#c14). Relevant passages: introduction lines 41–143; design 144–224; Chores, Hearts, and Things 229–397; exploration and discussion 398–576.

**Question and mechanism.** Can a small community govern recurring resource and coordination problems using lightweight feedback mechanisms while retaining human judgment? Chores uses a fixed point budget, increasing rewards for neglected work, and comparative priority judgments. Hearts uses sanctions together with replenishment toward a baseline, so mistakes need not become permanent exclusion. Things turns heterogeneous proposed purchases into explicit allocation choices. The mechanism is not “automate all governance”: operational activity, adjustment of rules, leadership, and in-person deliberation complement one another. The authors acknowledge the difficulty of separating mechanisms from participants and leadership. The abstract and discussion refer to different duration windows, so this should not be presented as a clean randomized field identification.

**Our model handles / misses.** Fixed thresholds and frozen testing provide a clear operational layer. The study lacks an explicit layer that allocates research attention or temporarily reduces risk after evidence of failure, then restores it. Its support diagnostics are informative but not calibrated decision rules. A declining score is not automatically a sufficient reason to retrain.

**Pre-data experiment.** Compare a fixed policy, a finite-memory violation-budget governor with cooldown and restoration, and an otherwise identical governor without restoration. Charge the budget only when labels have matured. Use fast-decay onset, recovery, feed gaps, and intermittent false alarms. Predefine restoration rules on development worlds. Measure loss before detection, drawdown, missed profitable opportunities, time to recovery, action oscillation, and permanent withdrawal. Compare with exposure- and turnover-matched random abstention.

**Falsification and rival.** Reject the governor's claimed informational benefit if it cannot beat matched abstention or repeatedly re-enters before recovery. Lower loss may simply reflect trading less. The constructive contribution is bounded memory and explicit policy levels, not a claim that house governance directly proves a trading controller stable.

### 14. Artificially intelligent agents in the social and behavioral sciences: A history and outlook

Petter Holme and Milena Tsvetkova. [Source](coverage.md#c15). Relevant passages: simulation and executable mechanisms 99–250; systems and symbolic approaches 252–543; cybernetics and complexity 558–830; data-driven approaches 831–1005; generative agents 1006–1240; conclusions 1265–1348.

**Question and mechanism.** What has been gained and lost as artificial agents have moved among explanation, simulation, behavioral prediction, and optimization? An executable agent mechanism forces a verbal theory to specify missing transitions. Simple interacting rules can generate aggregate phenomena; sophistication of an agent does not automatically make the aggregate model explanatory. The history distinguishes agents designed to perform well from agents designed to reproduce human behavior. Generative agents introduce reproducibility and behavioral-validation questions, including changing foundation models. Observation and instruments can themselves alter the system being studied.

**Our model handles / misses.** The stochastic simulator is a precise constructive mechanism with valuable accounting identities. It is not currently an agent-based explanation of how participant incentives produce persistent pressure. It is legitimate to use it without pretending otherwise. Adding an LLM to play a dealer would not by itself remedy identification.

**Pre-data experiment.** Construct two market worlds matched on selected TRAIN/CAL aggregate statistics: the present exogenous persistent-pressure world and a small heterogeneous-agent world with finite inventory, delayed execution, and bounded response to prices. Use simple transparent rules initially. Freeze models, cross-test them on both worlds, and intervene separately on pressure persistence and agent reaction speed. Compare forecast loss and the response signatures of flow autocorrelation, replenishment, impact decay, and cross-market propagation.

**Falsification and rival.** If the same forecast advantage survives both generative mechanisms without distinct intervention signatures, it identifies a useful observable regularity, not one unique microfoundation. If agent interactions destroy the advantage even when coarse moments match, the missing interaction structure matters. This experiment tests equifinality rather than demanding realism by description.

### 15. Strategic Classification

Moritz Hardt, Nimrod Megiddo, Christos Papadimitriou, and Mary Wootters. [Source](coverage.md#c16). Relevant passages: definitions 115–232; separable-cost analysis 235–665; general costs and computational difficulty 666–944; experiments and cost misspecification 949–1075; Appendix A 1189–1376.

**Question and mechanism.** How should a classifier be chosen when people can change their measured features after seeing the rule? A leader commits a classifier; a responding agent balances the gain in prediction against the cost of modifying features. Evaluation distinguishes the original underlying target from the modified observable. Under separable costs, the response geometry permits special learning reductions; broader costs are substantially harder. The assumptions about costs and rational response are doing real work. Learnability does not imply that a chosen cost model describes a real population or produces high absolute accuracy.

**Our model handles / misses.** It already separates observable states from latent causes. But observables such as flow, displayed depth, and timing are exogenous to the model's forecast and execution. The fixed threshold cannot yet be tested against responding agents. Feature robustness to missingness differs from robustness to strategically altered observations.

**Pre-data experiment.** In a sandbox, let cost-constrained synthetic liquidity/flow agents respond to published forecast thresholds by changing quote shading, timing, or displayed measurements while leaving the underlying fundamental innovation fixed. Sweep policy visibility, response fraction, reaction delay, heterogeneous costs, and bounded versus exact best response. Compare the unchanged predictor, a feature subset less directly affected by the response, and a response-aware policy trained only in development worlds.

**Falsification and rival.** Evaluate post-response proper loss, adverse excursion, and net results, including performance when assumed and actual response costs differ. A “robust” policy fails if its gain vanishes under modest cost misspecification or exposure matching. Build a nonstrategic control with the same marginal feature distortions but no dependency on the policy; that separates ordinary covariate shift from strategic response. Secrecy is not a substitute for this test.

### 16. Delayed Impact of Fair Machine Learning

Lydia T. Liu, Sarah Dean, Esther Rolf, Max Simchowitz, and Moritz Hardt. [Source](coverage.md#c17). Relevant passages: scope and limits 72–192; setup and outcome curve 193–325; constraints and main comparisons 330–510; measurement error and direct outcome optimization 511–577; threshold analysis 578–1119; simulations 1147–1244; conclusion 1304–1320; appendices 1379–2301.

**Question and mechanism.** Can a rule that meets a static fairness criterion worsen the later condition of the group it is intended to help? The construction separates the institution's utility from the individual's score change caused by selection. The expected score change is an outcome curve over selection rates. Under stated threshold and monotonicity assumptions, this curve permits comparison of unconstrained and fairness-constrained policies. Neither fairness nor utility maximization has a universal sign of long-run benefit. The baseline assumes zero average change under nonselection and discusses relaxing it. The core analysis is deliberately simpler than a complete many-period society.

**Our model handles / misses.** Forecast endpoint and path outcomes already differ, and the execution ledger imposes an opportunity cost through nonoverlapping positions. However, actions do not yet change future capital capacity, liquidity, or the set of feasible trades. The 60/120/240-minute forecasts model different maturation times, not the full delayed causal consequences of using them.

**Pre-data experiment.** Add a small capital-and-liquidity state whose transitions depend on realized trades and on nonparticipation. Compare a myopic expected-P&L policy, equal trade-rate rules across declared market regimes, and a policy constrained by long-run capital or tail outcomes. Start with a one-epoch outcome curve, then repeat epochs. Charge fees and adverse selection consistently; only matured outcomes update the state available to the learner. Track means, tail loss, ruin frequency, future opportunity count, and regime-conditional effects.

**Falsification and rival.** Reject a claimed long-run benefit if average gain masks capital depletion or failure concentrated in a stress regime. Compare equal exposure and turnover to test whether improvement is simply lower risk-taking. Market regimes are diagnostic strata here; they are not substitutes for demographic groups or a claim that the paper's normative fairness obligations transfer unchanged to markets.

### 17. A Broader View on Bias in Automated Decision-Making: Reflecting on Epistemology and Dynamics

Roel Dobbe, Sarah Dean, Thomas Gilbert, and Nitin Kohli. [Source](coverage.md#c18). Relevant passages: measurement, modeling, labels, and optimization 137–246; emergent effects 247–331; design implications 332–395.

**Question and mechanism.** Which choices create bias before a fitting algorithm is even selected, and how do those choices interact with deployment? The authors distinguish preexisting, technical, and emergent sources. A measured feature is an imperfect proxy for a chosen construct; labels and objectives encode choices. Closed-loop identification differs from learning an association on an unchanged population. Static performance can conceal multiple causal explanations.

**Our model handles / misses.** Defined units, valuation identities, receiver clocks, observable state labels, and explicit policy costs are strong epistemic choices. Remaining choices include the neutral band, aggregation clock, feature grouping, volatility normalization, horizon, and what the chosen target excludes. Their explanatory consequences deserve experiments, rather than treating them as neutral preprocessing.

**Pre-data experiment.** Reobserve the same latent synthetic paths through alternative bar aggregation, receiver latency, quote resolution, flow coverage, and label deadbands. Preserve chronological splits. Compare price-space targets with a carefully unit-consistent duration-coordinate target, holding the policy's economic exposure fixed. Record whether the claimed continuation sign and persistence survive each measurement change.

**Falsification and rival.** A mechanism claim weakens if it depends on a particular rounding or neutral-band choice while equivalent economic targets fail. A stable result across observation maps supports robustness of the construct but still does not identify the hidden cause uniquely. An apparent gain can be a measurement or selection artifact rather than better pressure measurement.

### 18. Performative Prediction

Juan C. Perdomo, Tijana Zrnic, Celestine Mendler-Dünner, and Moritz Hardt. [Source](coverage.md#c19). Relevant passages: distribution map and definitions 66–310; assumptions and repeated minimization 338–472; gradient updates 483–532; finite samples 533–608; stability versus optimality 609–648; strategic example 649–800; discussion 801–834; appendices 953–2029.

**Question and mechanism.** What happens when deployment changes the distribution on which a model will later be judged? Define a distribution map D(theta). A stable model minimizes loss on its own induced distribution held fixed. A performatively optimal model minimizes loss after allowing that distribution to change with the chosen model. These are different problems. The paper obtains a contraction for repeated risk minimization under sensitivity, smoothness, and strong-convexity assumptions: the key ratio is sensitivity times smoothness divided by strong convexity. Outside the assumptions there is no general guarantee. The base formulation is a fixed, memoryless map; deploying the same model repeatedly induces the same distribution.

**Our model handles / misses.** Frozen testing deliberately avoids this identification problem and remains a necessary reference. The existing stress worlds are exogenous shifts, not evidence of performative adaptation. Shallow boosted trees, finite path selection, and discrete actions do not automatically satisfy the paper's convex smooth theory.

**Pre-data experiment.** First use a small convex calibration or forecast head with a transparent action-response map. Then test the full model empirically. Let actions affect a liquidity premium or measured flow, with adjustable gain and delay. Compare frozen fitting, naive periodic retraining, damped updates, and regularized updates. Evaluate after deployment, across a grid of impact strengths, update intervals, and label delays. Include stateful persistence in a second stage rather than quietly treating it as the original memoryless map.

**Falsification and rival.** Measure cycles, parameter and prediction movement, calibration, tail losses, cumulative net results, and recovery. Convergence alone does not count as success; a stable policy can settle on poor outcomes. Compare paired worlds with the identical exogenous drift and innovations but the action-response link switched off. This separates model-induced change from ordinary nonstationarity. Local Jacobian or gain estimates can guide experiment design, but are not a universal stability certificate for the tree pipeline.

### 19. Breaking Feedback Loops in Recommender Systems with Causal Inference

Karl Krauth, Yixin Wang, and Michael I. Jordan. [Source](coverage.md#c20). Relevant passages: sequential model 162–344; causal formulation 345–484; identification and propensity weighting 487–629; positivity and additional assumptions 630–897; experiments 898–1179; appendix 1300–1392.

**Question and mechanism.** Can learning distinguish a user's preference from the system's own influence over what was observed? Past recommendations affect the data available to later recommenders. Causal adjusted feedback learning uses the sequential recommendation mechanism to reweight observations. This can correct selection even when underlying preferences themselves do not change. The extra identification route when ordinary positivity fails depends on strong structure such as stationarity, noninterference, and historical support. It is not a general license to infer outcomes of never-observed actions.

**Our model handles / misses.** Public quote outcomes can be observed whether or not this strategy trades, while fills, slippage, and private execution labels are action-selective. Current frozen training does not recycle those labels, so the specific learning loop is absent. Keeping unavailable forecast outcomes visible is already a valuable discipline. A future adaptive execution model must additionally distinguish “not traded,” “traded but label pending,” and “outcome permanently unavailable.”

**Pre-data experiment.** Generate selective fill/depth observations conditional on state and policy. Compare naive updating, known-propensity weighting, and an independent randomized audit stream that changes what is measured without determining ordinary trading actions. Use simulator counterfactual outcomes only as an evaluation oracle, kept inaccessible to inference. Log model version, action probabilities, eligibility, and outcome arrival time. Intentionally violate stationarity and shared-liquidity independence in separate worlds.

**Falsification and rival.** Under the declared identification assumptions, weighting should recover the oracle target within prespecified Monte Carlo error; failure rejects the implementation or assumptions. Under violations, expose bias, effective sample size, and extreme weights. A narrowing path distribution may arise from selection or bank truncation rather than a genuine reduction in market diversity. Better causal correction need not maximize every diversity metric.

### 20. Data Feedback Loops: Model-driven Amplification of Dataset Biases

Rohan Taori and Tatsunori B. Hashimoto. [Source](coverage.md#c21). Relevant passages: model 145–208; calibration and bound 209–426; experiments 427–798; examples and implementation 1019–1764; ablations 1770–2113; proof and conjectural component 2114–2376.

**Question and mechanism.** How do biases evolve when a model's outputs enter the next model's training data? The construction mixes fresh base-distribution labels with model-generated labels. Hard decoding can convert modest probability differences into an imbalanced output stream. The theoretical stability condition is consistent calibration across a specified family of distributions, not merely a good reliability diagram on one held-out set. With fresh-data mass and nonincreasing calibration error, the expected amplification bound scales with the ratio of total incoming data to fresh base data. Its proof keeps the covariate marginal fixed; its distributional-generalization step invokes a stated conjecture. The bound is not a per-run promise.

**Our model handles / misses.** The path bank contains observed synthetic future paths from TRAIN, not recursively relabeled predictions, so “model collapse” is not a demonstrated current defect. But recursive pseudo-labels, treating forecast-filled missing prices as observations, or retaining only confidently predicted states in future updates would create the relevant channel. Finite support already prevents the bank from inventing unseen tail shapes; that is distinct from recursive amplification.

**Pre-data experiment.** Contaminate a sandbox path bank or training target stream with controlled fractions of model-produced labels. Compare hard class labels, probability sampling, and independent fresh simulator outcomes; keep total bank capacity and update counts matched. Include a no-recursion control with the same quantization and data volume. Measure rare-tail frequency, state occupancy, entropy, proper loss, conditional calibration, and economic tail risk over rounds. Include the limit of no fresh independent labels explicitly; the stated bound cannot be extended through a zero denominator.

**Falsification and rival.** Reject a stability claim if aggregate calibration looks good while rare conditional tails disappear. Separate finite-bank and decoding artifacts from recursive amplification. The paper's language-model experiment also shows that stabilizing one frequency can coincide with much worse generalization and copying; therefore blindly overfitting the CAD model is not the lesson.

### 21. A Classification of Feedback Loops and Their Relation to Biases in Automated Decision-Making Systems

Nicolò Pagan, Joachim Baumann, Ezzat Elokda, Giulia De Pasquale, Saverio Bolognani, and Anikó Hannák. [Source](coverage.md#c22). Relevant passages: system representation 87–132; taxonomy 133–293; stability 294–315; bias mapping 316–386; simulations 387–585; discussion 586–661; search method and extensions 834–880.

**Question and mechanism.** Where exactly does a decision re-enter an ML pipeline? Five channels are distinguished: sampling, underlying individual characteristics, measured features, outcomes, and model-development data. A strategic response is a special behavioral mechanism, not a sixth destination. A model-training loop exists when decisions gate the data; chronological accumulation independent of decisions is an open-loop system with memory (lines 221–232). The simulations include feedback that reduces measurement bias as well as feedback that reinforces it. Stable equilibria can be undesirable. The taxonomy is grounded in the reviewed examples and explicitly open to finer subdivisions.

**Our model handles / misses.** The simulator has internal temporal dependence, but the strategy does not currently close these decision-to-world links. Logged discrepancy and subsequent human research are a different, slower loop. Calling all three “feedback” without specifying the arrow obscures what has been tested.

**Pre-data experiment.** Build a five-switch synthetic test matrix: actions change available opportunities; actions change persistent liquidity/inventory state; actions distort only measured features; actions change realized endpoint or execution outcome; actions gate future training examples. Initially enable one at a time, then pair the strongest two. Preserve paired random innovations. Log both latent and observed states for evaluation, while exposing only observations to the strategy.

**Falsification and rival.** A diagnostic that claims to identify a particular channel should fail its claim if it reacts identically to a matched exogenous perturbation or misattributes selective observation to changing fundamentals. Some feedback may improve observations; therefore the desired result is a causal map of effects, not a universal finding that feedback is bad. Negative feedback is not a synonym for beneficial behavior or guaranteed stability.

### 22. Performative Prediction: Past and Future

Moritz Hardt and Celestine Mendler-Dünner. [Source](coverage.md#c23). Relevant passages: historical fixed-point construction 95–125; framework 126–257; retraining, deployment schedules, and dynamic benchmarks 262–626; risk optimization 627–776; causal identification and microfoundations 777–925; performative power 926–1150; collective action 1151–1243; limits 1244–1294.

**Question and mechanism.** What does it mean to predict well when a prediction can influence the target? The survey separates learning patterns in a fixed distribution from steering the distribution itself. Continuous response on a bounded domain can admit a self-consistent public prediction, but existence is not an algorithm for reaching the point. Repeated training seeks stability, not necessarily minimum post-deployment risk. Public deployments and private optimization updates are different clocks. The survey also distinguishes parameter-space sensitivity, prediction-mediated effects, and the difficulty of identifying effects from deterministic continuous predictions. Performative power measures how strongly available interventions can move outcomes; zero power recovers a price-taking interpretation.

**Our model handles / misses.** The current private one-contract design is a coherent zero-impact baseline. It has no estimate of how the result changes as visibility, adoption, or size grows. Forecasts alone do not reveal whether a subsequently correct prediction discovered movement or helped produce it. The fixed state ontology also does not cover every philosophical sense of reflexivity; the survey explicitly limits its own formalism.

**Pre-data experiment.** Sweep dissemination and adoption independently of order size. Compare a private forecast, a public forecast observed but not acted upon, agents responding to the public forecast, and direct impact from the strategy's own actions. In paired worlds decompose the change in risk into fit on the original distribution and the effect of changed conditions. Add small randomized forecast or policy perturbations inside the synthetic environment to test whether an estimated response model recovers the known structural effect. Compare greedy deployment with batched private updates at equal total sample budgets and matured-label availability.

**Falsification and rival.** Reject a claimed predictive-information gain if it appears only when the policy steers outcomes and disappears for an untouched observer. Reject a response estimate if it cannot recover the simulator's known effect under its own identification design. Apparent equilibrium may be an echo chamber, and rational representative-agent responses may exaggerate discontinuities; test heterogeneous noisy responses too. A dynamic failure-case benchmark is useful for development, but it should coexist with a sealed evaluation suite.

### 23. Tackling Interference Induced by Data Training Loops in A/B Tests: A Weighted Training Approach

Nian Si. [Source](coverage.md#c24). Relevant passages: pooled-learning interference 47–114; potential outcomes and global treatment effect 213–383; weighting 384–532; simulations 533–795; proofs 977–1029; additional A/A results 1095–1114.

**Question and mechanism.** Does an A/B comparison still estimate the effect of deploying either policy globally if both arms train on a shared pool of action-generated observations? The pooled training distribution can differ from either full-deployment world. The proposed weights use the probability that an observed feature vector came from an arm, normalized by arm allocation; under the specified conditional-independence assumption they recover each arm's data distribution. If treatment directly changes outcomes beyond features, outcome information must enter the weight model. The theorem minimizes the second moment of weights among exact distribution-recovering weights. This is narrower than certifying the final adaptive treatment-effect estimator's uncertainty.

**Our model handles / misses.** Separate frozen fits avoid this particular shared-training contamination. A future comparison of two adaptive horizons or feature policies could reintroduce it if they share a fitted execution model, path bank, or calibration pool. Sharing random innovations between independent simulated worlds is beneficial variance reduction; sharing endogenous learned state is a different operation.

**Pre-data experiment.** Construct independent all-policy-A and all-policy-B synthetic worlds as a known global-effect target. Compare pooled A/B learning, frozen snapshots, separated training, and weighted training inside a mixed world. Match training volume and update schedules. Repeat under shared liquidity spillovers, which weighting of training data alone cannot generally remove. Run genuine A/A experiments and evaluate empirical confidence-interval coverage over independent world replications.

**Falsification and rival.** The paper's Table 5 reports 45% false rejection for one weighted-method metric at nominal 5%; Appendix Table 10 reports 47% (lines 762–779 and 1103–1109). Thus lower bias is insufficient. Reject any proposed inferential method that misses its predeclared A/A error tolerance. A difference can arise from reduced batch size or learner variance, rather than the trading policy itself. This is a direct reason to test inferential behavior before adding adaptive comparisons.

### 24. Monitoring the performance of machine learning algorithms that induce feedback loops: what is the causal estimand?

Jean Feng, Adarsh Subbaswamy, Alexej Gossmann, Harvineet Singh, Berkman Sahiner, Mi-Ok Kim, Gene Pennello, Nicholas Petrick, Romain Pirracchio, and Fan Xia. [Source](coverage.md#c25). Relevant passages: monitoring goals 80–130; sequential formulation 153–195; three criteria 196–273; causal data model 274–323; monitoring procedures 324–526; simulations 527–582; discussion 583–611; control limits and simulation assumptions 854–934.

**Question and mechanism.** What exactly should an alarm detect when actions taken because of a forecast alter the observed result? Standalone counterfactual prediction quality and overall outcome improvement are different estimands. Average predictive values, subgroup values, and excessive confidence can produce different detection delays and assumptions. Sequential weighting can identify some targets under positivity and conditional exchangeability. A residual-based criterion may avoid positivity for Type I error control but still have poor power in rarely observed regions. The simulations simplify propensity estimation and calibrate some comparisons under a stronger null; those conditions matter when interpreting performance.

**Our model handles / misses.** The study publishes distance, entropy, coverage, and effective scenario size but correctly does not call them calibrated abstention rules. The missing piece is a declared monitor target with measured false alarms, delay, and action consequences. Eight TEST sessions and heavily overlapping forecasts cannot simply be treated as hundreds of independent alarm opportunities. Longer horizons create delayed feedback and a larger pending-outcome backlog.

**Pre-data experiment.** Define three monitors: passive forecast proper loss; regime-conditional endpoint/path-risk miscalibration; and deterioration in the total executed policy outcome. Fit alarm rules only on development worlds. Simulate no-change, abrupt fast decay, gradual change, isolated illiquidity, observation failure, and effective intervention that reduces the event the forecast anticipated. Update scores only when the requisite labels mature. Compare aggregate and subgroup residual charts with calibrated sequential thresholds. Measure probability of any false alarm over a declared monitoring window, detection delay in minutes and matured observations, losses before detection, and restart behavior.

**Falsification and rival.** Reject a monitor that fires in stable null worlds too often or interprets successful risk reduction as predictor failure. A fast alarm may exploit initial miscalibration, not detect a new change. A quiet monitor may simply lack observations in the failing region. Independent audit observations can improve identification, but their value depends on the target and sampling allocation; randomization is not universally faster.

### 25. Towards a Systems Theory of Algorithms

Florian Dörfler, Zhiyu He, Giuseppe Belgioioso, Saverio Bolognani, John Lygeros, and Michael Muehlebach. [Source](coverage.md#c26). Relevant passages: open algorithms and recursive estimation 75–208; position 209–307; analysis tools and examples 321–594; real-time interconnections 595–923; layered architectures 924–1059; challenges 1060–1198.

**Question and mechanism.** How should an algorithm be analyzed when it is a stateful, imperfect, real-time component connected to other algorithms and to an environment? The useful abstraction has inputs, outputs, internal state, and disturbances. Recursive least squares illustrates that an online causal realization can correspond to a batch estimation problem while having different operational properties. Examples use feedback, disturbance gains, time-scale separation, and transient objectives. Small-gain and related arguments concern composed systems under explicit assumptions, not a claim that every assembled pipeline is stable. Layering can impose useful structure but also create bias or hide coupled objectives.

**Our model handles / misses.** The causal observation pipeline, deployment snapshots, and missing-data audit already implement part of this view. Missing operational experiments include algorithm latency, irregular update clocks, stale model states, partial computations, and the interaction between prediction, risk gating, execution, monitoring, and retraining. The finite scenario bank and separate horizons are algorithmic state choices with their own memory and consistency properties.

**Pre-data experiment.** Draw the pipeline as stateful blocks and inject controlled perturbations at each interface: delayed messages, timestamp jitter, reduced path-bank support, bounded forecast perturbations, delayed monitor alarms, and batched model updates. Sweep market reaction speed against inference, execution, monitoring, and retraining speeds. Compare a modular predictor-plus-governor with a coupled decision-aware design at equal compute and information budgets. Estimate local disturbance amplification and transient regret; include abrupt threshold crossings where a local smooth approximation fails.

**Falsification and rival.** Reject an architectural advantage if it disappears under equal latency or compute, or if individually stable blocks generate repeated order/abstention cycles when connected. A faster optimizer is not necessarily a better decision system; it can react to noise or stale labels faster. The experiment tests a mathematical construction through measurable input/output behavior, without requiring the market to possess a literal physical energy.

## An ordered research program that needs no real dataset

1. **Resolve component attribution.** Freeze the existing run as historical evidence. On new prespecified synthetic development and evaluation worlds, compare the endpoint head, each path expert, their mixture, and a direct baseline.
